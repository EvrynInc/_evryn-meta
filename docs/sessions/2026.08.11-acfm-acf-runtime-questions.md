## Q1 🔴 Do the agents have a session, or do they wake fresh every turn?

**Justin's hypothesis:** *"You've got the whole thread… but that's kind of it. You don't have access to your thinking during that turn, or any of the other deep layers — just the superficial layer. The chat forms an ongoing transcript, but you don't have a session."*

### WHAT THE CODE DOES — **Justin is exactly right. There is no session. Confirmed at source.**

`src/wakes/index.ts:417-434` is the single place the runtime calls the model. It passes a `systemPrompt`, a `prompt`, a model id, tools and hooks — **and no `resume`, no `sessionId`, no `continue`.** I swept the whole file for those terms; the only hits are Slack's `/resume` command, which is a completely unrelated pause/park feature. **Every wake is a brand-new conversation with the model.**

**So here is the honest ledger of what survives from one wake to the next, and what does not:**

**Survives** (because the runtime deliberately re-composes it every time):
- Your identity, your `MEMORY.md`, team current-state, skills — the stable layers.
- **The thread transcript in full**, including your own past replies — because a reply is written into `agent_messages` and re-composed next time.
- Any **note** you deliberately wrote to memory during the wake.
- Digest blurbs of your other rooms, and your private scratchpad for this room.

**Does NOT survive — this is the part Justin identified:**
- **Your reasoning.** Everything you thought and did not say is gone.
- **Your tool calls and their results.** If you ran `search_messages` and read fifty messages, or `read_thread` on another room, or `load_skill` — **none of it comes back.** You get the conclusion only if you wrote one down.
- **Anything you loaded mid-wake.**
- **Every intermediate turn.** `MAX_TURNS = 40` (`wakes/index.ts:183`), so a wake can be a rich forty-step agentic loop internally — and **all forty steps are discarded** except the final structured output's `reply` and `notes`.

**⇒ Justin's word for it — "a disaster" — is a fair description of one specific thing:** an agent that spent a wake investigating something and concluded *"nothing here yet, I'll watch it"* has, by the next wake, **no memory of having investigated at all.** It cannot know what it already ruled out. That is a real and recurring cost, and it is the strongest argument in this whole document.

### Should we use the SDK's sessions? — RECOMMENDATION

**Why we moved away** (design §3.2, ADR-052): SDK/auto compaction summarizes history **opaquely and un-steerably** — a summarizer whose output we do not control, silently shaving precision. Fresh composition keeps every byte enumerable, logged in the wake manifest, and diffable.

**Does the objection still hold under tight membership?** **Partly, and less than it did — but the reasoning has a hole worth naming.**

- **Justin's point is genuinely strong.** Compaction only fires when a context grows large. Step 4 exists precisely to keep rooms small, and Step 5 ages them out at seven days. **Under tight membership, most threads should never reach compaction at all** — so the objection would apply to a minority of conversations rather than all of them.
- **His mitigation is also sound as far as it goes:** a standing order to announce compaction converts a silent event into a loud one.
- **⚠️ But here is what I do not think has been reckoned with, and it is the reason I would not simply adopt it:** an announcement tells you compaction *happened*. It cannot tell you *what was lost* — that is the whole nature of the failure. And a session's contents are **not enumerable in the wake manifest**, which is the artifact that makes every composition diffable and is one of this runtime's genuinely unusual strengths. **Adopting sessions trades a known, complete, inspectable prompt for an opaque accumulating one.** That is a real architectural loss, not a preference.
- **Also unverified by me and load-bearing: I do not know whether the SDK exposes a session in a form we could persist per (agent, thread) across process restarts.** The whole runtime is built on "everything durable is in Postgres; crash → restart → re-arm losslessly." A session living in SDK-side state would be the first thing in the system that isn't. **Somebody has to check that before this is designable.** I am flagging it as UNKNOWN rather than guessing.

**⇒ My recommendation: do NOT swap the model wholesale, and do NOT dismiss the problem either — because the problem Justin identified is real and the cheaper fix targets it directly.**

1. **First, close the actual pain without sessions.** The specific loss that hurts is *reasoning and tool-work vanishing*. A **wake-scoped working record** — the agent's own scratchpad, which already exists per (agent, thread) and already composes back into that room — is the natural home. **Today nothing instructs the agent to use it that way.** Making "before you finish, put in your scratchpad what you looked at and what you ruled out" a standing instruction recovers most of the lost value at **zero architectural cost**, and it is steerable, inspectable, and already built. **This is the cheap 80%.**
2. **Then, if that proves insufficient, trial sessions on ONE agent in ONE long-running thread** and measure. Not fleet-wide, not by default.
3. **Answer the persistence question first** (the UNKNOWN above). If a session cannot survive a restart, the trial design changes completely.

## Q2 The digest semantics — confirmed, with one correction

**Justin's statement:** *in thread A you get no digest of A; in thread B you get A's digest; thread C you have never been in, so you are entirely oblivious to it.*

### WHAT THE CODE DOES — **all three are correct, and all three are deliberate.**

- **In A, you get no digest of A.** `src/composer/index.ts:176` filters the current thread out of the digest list. **The reason is not tidiness:** you are holding A's *entire transcript* in this wake, so a summary of it would be strictly worse information, and — more importantly — composing it would let a busy room trigger its own summary regeneration on every wake. Excluding it makes that loop **structurally impossible** rather than merely discouraged.
- **In B, you get A's digest.** Same line: every room you are a member of, minus the one you're in.
- **You are oblivious to C.** Correct, and it is the entire point of the design. The digest list is built from **your own membership rows** (`composer/index.ts:169`). No membership, no digest, no awareness. **This is the cost lever:** obliviousness to rooms you are not in is what makes eight agents affordable.

**One correction to the framing:** it is *ever*-membership, not current. **If you leave a room, you keep seeing its digest** — deliberately, so that stepping out is cheap and not a form of amnesia. You stop being *woken*; you keep being *aware*. And when the room finally ages out, you still get its closing memory.

**⚠️ And the safety net that makes obliviousness acceptable is NOT BUILT YET.** The design's answer to "what about rooms you're not in" is the **little birds** — a cheap daily pass over channels you are not in, pinging you only when something affects you. **They are unbuilt** (tracker Step 39). Until they exist, obliviousness to C is total and has no backstop. **That should be said out loud whenever this design is described, because it is the load-bearing caveat.**

### RECOMMENDED `ARCHITECTURE.md` WORDING

> **Ambient awareness is scoped to membership, and the scoping is the cost model.**
>
> An agent's wake composes the **full transcript of the room it is in**, plus a **one-line digest of every other room it is a member of** — and nothing at all about rooms it has never been in.
>
> - **The current room is deliberately excluded from the digest layer.** Its transcript is already loaded in full, so a summary would be strictly worse information — and excluding it makes it structurally impossible for a busy room to trigger its own digest regeneration on every wake.
> - **Digest visibility follows *ever*-membership, not current membership.** Leaving a room stops you being woken there; it does not stop you seeing it. You keep its digest until the room ages out, and you receive its closing memory when it does.
> - **A room you have never joined is invisible.** There is no ambient signal, no summary, no notification. This is the wake-economics lever: an eight-agent team is affordable only because most agents are in most rooms *not at all*.
> - ⚠️ **The intended counterweight — the "little birds," a cheap daily pass over the rooms you are not in — is not built.** Until it is, obliviousness has no backstop, and membership must be managed deliberately rather than casually.

## Q3 `subscribe` vs `member` — the same thing, and we should stop using two words

### WHAT THE CODE DOES

**They are one mechanism.** There is a single table, `thread_subscriptions`. A row with `dismissed_at` **null** means *you are in the room and will be woken*. A row with `dismissed_at` **set** means *you were in the room, you are not woken any more, and you still see its digest*.

"Subscribe" is the older word — it comes from the Slack `@mention → subscribe` path (`src/slack/index.ts:114-125`), which predates the membership model. "Member" is the word the newer design and the new verbs use. **They name the same row.**

### RECOMMENDATION — **use "member" everywhere; retire "subscribe" from agent-facing text.**

"Subscribe" carries the wrong mental model: it sounds like a newsletter — passive, low-stakes, something you opt into for updates. **Membership is the opposite: it is the thing that decides whether you are woken at all, and it is the single largest cost lever in the system.** "Member" also makes the social practice legible — you are *in the room* or you are not — which is exactly how the design intends agents and Justin to reason about it.

Keep "subscription" only where it names the database table, in code and comments. **In `runtime-ops.md` and every agent-facing description, say member / join / leave / invite.**

## Q4 `/dismiss` — who does it dismiss? **And a correction to a premise**

### WHAT THE CODE DOES (`src/slack/index.ts:239-263`)

- **You do NOT type `/dismiss @Lucas`.** The command takes **no agent argument**. Each agent runs as **its own Slack app** with its own bot token, and the handler dismisses **the agent whose app received the command** — bound when the app is constructed.
- **`/dismiss <thread_ts>`** dismisses that one thread.
- **`/dismiss` with no argument dismisses that agent from EVERY live thread in the current channel** — a channel-wide exit. Worth knowing; it is a bigger hammer than it looks.

**🔴 CORRECTION TO A PREMISE, and it matters for Q5:** it has been assumed that `/dismiss` is *visible* because it "lands in the transcript." **It does not.** The handler answers with `respond(...)` (`:244`, `:259`), which for a Slack slash command is **ephemeral by default — only the person who typed it sees it.** Nothing is posted to the channel and nothing is written to the thread. So `/dismiss` is visible **to Justin, because he typed it** — and invisible to everyone else, exactly like `leave_thread`.

**⇒ So the visibility gap is wider than diagnosed: BOTH exit paths are silent to the room.**

### RECOMMENDATION

**Yes, we will need per-agent addressing, and it is a Phase-3 blocker rather than a nicety.** Slack allows one app to own a given slash-command name per workspace, so with eight agents each running its own app, **`/dismiss` cannot be registered by all eight** — whichever app owns it is the only one it can ever reach. Options: one command per agent (`/dismiss-lucas`), or a single ops app owning `/dismiss <agent> [thread]`. **I recommend the latter** — one command, an explicit agent argument, no ambiguity about who you just dismissed, and it composes with the `/who` proposal in Q5. **This needs deciding before a second agent stamps on, not after.**

## Q5 Making leaving LOUD — the cheapest option

**Justin's concern, exactly:** *"if I say 'thanks Lucas, that's all I need' and I just mean for this topic and he takes that as his cue to exit, I want to know that."*

### WHAT THE CODE DOES

`leave_thread` writes one database update and nothing else (`src/tools/tools.ts:561-569`). No message, no Slack post. Per Q4, `/dismiss` is ephemeral. **The only two `chat.postMessage` calls in the entire runtime are an agent's own reply and the test-channel post.** Nothing announces a departure to anyone.

**And the constraint that rules out the obvious fix:** posting a *message* into the thread would (a) trip the activity trigger and reset the room's dormancy clock, and (b) **fan out and wake every remaining member at roughly 64,000 tokens each — so every goodbye wakes the whole room.** That directly fights the cost lever Step 4 exists to be.

### RECOMMENDATION — **the cheapest loud option is to say it in the reply the agent is already sending**

**Do not build a notification. Change what the agent says.**

An agent calling `leave_thread` is, in almost every real case, mid-wake and about to send a reply anyway — *"Happy to help, I'll step out."* **That reply is already posted to Slack and already costs nothing extra.** The gap is not that the runtime lacks a channel; it is that **nothing instructs the agent to say it out loud.**

**⇒ Recommendation 5.a (do this): a `runtime-ops.md` beat — "when you leave a room, say so in the room, in the same turn."** Zero code, zero wakes, zero cost, and it lands in the Slack transcript where Justin is already looking. **It is not enforceable**, which is its one real weakness — the agent could leave silently. But it converts the common case from invisible to visible for free.

**⇒ Recommendation 5.b (do this too, and it is the one that actually answers his question): make membership *checkable* — a `/who` command.** Justin's deeper need is not to be notified of every exit; it is to be able to **ask who is here** when he comes back to a thread. That is a small command handler over a query the runtime already runs. **A check he can perform on demand is strictly more valuable than a notification he might miss** — and it also covers silent joiners and members who have never spoken, which no departure notice ever would.

**⇒ 5.c (only if 5.a proves unreliable): a structural announcement that does not fan out** — a message row written with a type the wake fan-out skips, so it appears in the transcript and in future compositions but wakes nobody. **Real work, and it needs care** to avoid resetting the dormancy clock. Hold it in reserve.

**Ranking: 5.b first (it is the actual answer), 5.a immediately alongside it (free), 5.c only on evidence.**

## Q6 Does a departed agent know it left?

**Justin:** *"will they know they were a member? or will that just be some disembodied convo that they don't know why they're not a part of?"*

### WHAT THE CODE DOES — **no, and this is a real gap nobody considered**

Nothing in a composed wake names the agent's own membership state. The digest layer shows the room's blurb **identically whether the agent is a current member or a leaver** — because digest visibility follows *ever*-membership. `leave_thread` writes no memory note and leaves no trace in the agent's own context.

**⇒ So the disembodied experience Justin describes is exactly what happens today.** An agent sees a room's digest updating, has no signal that it is no longer in that room, and — because it also cannot see the member list (Q1's sibling gap) — **has no way to find out.** If it wondered "why am I not being woken there?", the runtime offers no answer.

**⚠️ And a sharper version, worth stating because it is the one that will actually bite:** the agent that left is *also* the agent that decided to leave — but by the next wake it has **no memory of the decision** (Q1: reasoning does not survive). So it cannot distinguish *"I chose to step out of that room"* from *"I was dismissed"* from *"I was never in it."* Those are three very different situations and it sees one undifferentiated digest line for all of them.

### RECOMMENDATION — **Justin's second instinct is the right one: a query, not a log**

He proposed a memory entry per departure and then immediately doubted it. **His doubt is correct and I would not build the log.** A per-departure memory entry is an append-only list that grows forever, goes stale the moment the agent is re-invited, and — because memory composes into *every* wake in *every* room — **it taxes every future wake to record something relevant to almost none of them.** That is precisely the memory-bloat pattern the scratchpad was built to relieve.

**⇒ Build the query.** The infrastructure Step 4 added already answers it: current membership is one indexed read. Two small pieces:

1. **Compose the agent's own membership state into the digest layer** — mark each room it is *not* currently a member of. One extra field on data already being read; it sits below the cache-stable prefix so it costs no cache. **This alone ends the disembodiment**: the agent looks at its digest list and can see which rooms it is in.
2. **Optionally, a `who_is_in` tool** so an agent can ask about a specific room. Lower priority than (1) — (1) answers the question passively, without the agent needing to think to ask.

**On "should the agent get a chance to decide what it does now it only sees digests":** I would **not** build a special post-leaving moment. It adds a wake to save a wake. If (1) is composed, the agent naturally sees its own status at its next wake in any room and can act then, at no extra cost. **Prefer ambient truth over a ceremony.**

## Q7 Rewriting the "you never need to message yourself" line

**Current text (§4a of the proposed `runtime-ops.md` edit):** *"you never need to message yourself, because a note is already delivered to every future you."*

**Justin: *"this could get mis-read."*** He is right, and here is precisely how. The sentence is true of **one** situation and false or misleading in **three others**:

1. ✅ **True:** something you learn in room A that a future you in room B will need. A memory note genuinely delivers that.
2. ❌ **Misleading — timing.** "Delivered to every future you" sounds instant. It is delivered **at your next wake**, which may be days away, and **only when something wakes you**. A note is not a reminder and will not summon you. **`wakeup_set` is the thing that summons you.**
3. ❌ **Misleading — scope.** Working state for *this* room does not belong in memory at all; it belongs in the scratchpad, which composes back into this room only. Reading the line as "notes are how I talk to myself" invites exactly the memory bloat we are trying to prevent.
4. ❌ **False — reaching another room.** If something needs to happen in room B, a note does **not** get it there. Nobody in B wakes. `post_to_thread` is the verb that reaches another room.

### RECOMMENDED REPLACEMENT TEXT

> **Reaching your future self, and reaching another room — these are three different tools, and using the wrong one silently does nothing.**
>
> - **A note in your memory** is for something a future you, *in a different room*, will need — a decision, a commitment, a durable fact. It arrives **at your next wake, whenever that happens to be**. It is not a reminder and it will not wake you.
> - **`wakeup_set`** is how you make something happen *at a time*. If it must happen Tuesday, a note will not do it — schedule the wakeup.
> - **`post_to_thread`** is how you reach *another room*. A note reaches only you; posting wakes the people who are actually there.
> - **Your scratchpad** is for working state in *this* room — where you are, what you are tracking, what you are waiting on. It comes back the next time you are here, and it does not follow you elsewhere. **Working scratch belongs here, never in memory.**
>
> **So: you never need to send yourself a message — but only because these three tools already cover the three real cases. Pick the one that matches what you actually need to happen.**

## Q8 Making the memory guidance precise

**The problem line:** *"your memory is the real channel between them."* Justin is right that it reads as **write more**.

**⚠️ Q1 changes this answer, and it is worth saying plainly:** with no session, an agent genuinely *cannot* recover its own reasoning, so there is a real and rational pull toward over-writing — writing things down is the only defense it has. **Telling it to write less without giving it somewhere better to put things is an instruction it will quietly disobey, and it would be right to.** So the guidance below only works because it **routes** rather than restricts: every "don't put it in memory" comes with a "put it here instead."

### RECOMMENDED REPLACEMENT TEXT

> **Before you write anything down, ask two questions.**
>
> **1. Will I need this outside this room, or only inside it?**
> - **Only inside it** → your **scratchpad**. Where you got to, what you are tracking, what you are waiting on, what you already checked and ruled out. It comes back every time you are here and follows you nowhere else. **This is most of what you will want to write.**
> - **Outside it** → keep reading.
>
> **2. Is this really mine to hold, or does it belong in a document?**
> - **A decision, a standard, a process, a fact the team relies on** → it belongs in the **project doc or the protocol**, where everyone gets it and it can be corrected. Say so in the room, or propose the edit. **Do not privately memorize something that should be written down for everybody** — that is how a team ends up with eight divergent versions of one fact.
> - **Genuinely yours** — a commitment you gave, a judgment you formed, a working relationship, something you learned the hard way → **memory**, and keep it short.
>
> **What memory is for, stated precisely: memory is mostly POINTERS, not contents.** *"Agreed the pricing model with Emma in thread 1234 — she owns the numbers"* is worth more than a reconstruction of the discussion, because **the thread is still there and its digest leads you back to it.** Nothing said in a room is ever lost; you can always read it again. What you cannot recover is *that it mattered*, and *where to look*. **That is the thing to write.**
>
> **The test:** if a future you in a different room would be worse off without it → write it. If a future you would simply re-read the thread → **do not write it; the digest already points there.**

## Q9 Dormant → revive must restore last state

**Justin's requirement:** *"if they were in the room when the room went dormant, they are in the room when the room comes out of dormancy."*

### WHAT THE CODE DOES — **membership survives; visibility does not. Verified.**

- **Membership is safe.** I swept `src/digest/index.ts` — the entire dormancy path touches `thread_subscriptions` **only to read it** (`:104`, `:579`, `:652`). It never writes, updates, or deletes a membership row. **Going dormant does not remove anybody from anything.** Justin's requirement holds today for membership, structurally and for free.
- **Visibility does not survive, and cannot yet be restored.** Composition filters out rooms whose state is `dormant`, so the digest disappears — correctly, that is what ageing out means. **But nothing anywhere sets a thread back to `live`.** A dead room that comes back to life stays hidden from everyone's ambient view forever. That is finding **`L12`**, and it is real and open.

**Nothing is lost** — the transcript is intact, the closing memories are written, and an @mention pulls anyone straight back in. The gap is only the *passive* return.

### RECOMMENDED `L12` DESIGN — folding in Justin's requirement

1. **Revive in the dormancy sweep, not in the database trigger.** The sweep already runs on a cadence and already owns this lifecycle; a room whose activity has moved back inside the window returns to `live` there. Deterministic, testable, one place, no schema change. The cost is that revival lands up to one sweep interval late — and since what is being restored is a *passive digest line*, while an @mention already restores access instantly, that delay is invisible.
2. **🔴 Revival MUST also clear that room's dormancy-write completion records.** This is the obligation I found while building Step 5 and it is easy to miss. Those records mark *"this agent has written its closing memory for this room."* If a room half-closes (some members write their memory), revives, and later closes again, **every member already marked complete is skipped — they get no memory of the room's second life.** Clearing the records on revival is what makes a second closing produce a second, honest ending.
3. **Justin's requirement needs no extra work.** Because membership rows are untouched by dormancy, everyone who was in the room when it went quiet is still in it when it comes back — including leavers, who keep the digest as ever-members. **Revival restores the room; it does not need to restore the roster, because the roster never left.**
4. **One thing to decide, flagged not settled:** should a revived room *announce* itself — the design's "echo rule" says a returning digest line should be marked *"re-activated after dormancy,"* so an agent holding both a closing memory and a fresh digest line reads them as one continuing story rather than two unrelated things. **That marking is not built.** It is small and it is the difference between a coherent return and a confusing one.
